Critical Thinking: "Is This Correct?" Case

Analyze a real-world scenario to determine if a dosage calculation is accurate. Apply dimensional analysis and demonstrate clinical reasoning.

📋 Clinical Scenario
Patient: Mr. Johnson, 68-year-old male with hypertension
Order: Dr. Angio orders 0.25g of Drug X (an anti-hypertensive)
Available: The drug label reads "500mg per tablet"
MA Action: Heather, the new MA, calculates that she should give 2 tablets
Heather's Calculation:
0.25g × (1000mg/1g) = 250mg → 250mg ÷ 500mg/tablet = 0.5 tablets
Based on the calculation above, is Heather's conclusion (2 tablets) correct?
A
No, she should give 0.5 tablets (250mg ÷ 500mg/tablet)
B
Yes, 2 tablets is correct (0.25g = 250mg, so 2 tablets of 500mg)
C
She should give 1 tablet (500mg is close enough to 250mg)
Convert the provider's order (0.25g) to milligrams:
Using dimensional analysis, calculate how many tablets to administer:
📐 Dimensional Analysis Formula
Desired Dose (mg) ÷ Available Dose (mg/tablet) = Number of Tablets
250mg ÷ 500mg/tablet = ? tablets
💡 Critical Thinking Tip
Always double-check your units! In this case, the order was in grams (0.25g) but the available medication was in milligrams (500mg). Converting to the same unit before calculating prevents serious medication errors.
✓ Case Analysis Complete!
Correct Analysis: Heather's calculation was incorrect. She should administer 0.5 tablets.

Step-by-Step Reasoning:
1. Convert 0.25g to mg: 0.25g × 1000 = 250mg
2. Available: 500mg per tablet
3. Calculate: 250mg ÷ 500mg/tablet = 0.5 tablets

Clinical Significance: Giving 2 tablets (1000mg) would be 4 times the ordered dose, potentially causing severe hypotension, bradycardia, or other dangerous side effects in this hypertensive patient.

Error Identification & Ethical Responsibilities

Identify common dosage calculation errors and discuss the legal/ethical responsibilities of medical assistants when spotting potential errors.

🔍 Error Analysis #1: Misplaced Decimal
Scenario: Order: 0.125mg of Digoxin
Available: 0.25mg tablets
Incorrect Calculation:
0.125mg ÷ 0.25mg/tablet = 1.25 tablets (incorrect)
What's wrong with this calculation?
A
Nothing is wrong; 1.25 tablets is correct
B
0.125 ÷ 0.25 = 0.5 tablets (decimal was misplaced)
C
Should be 2 tablets (0.25 ÷ 0.125)
🔍 Error Analysis #2: Incorrect Conversion
Scenario: Order: 1.5g Amoxicillin
Available: 500mg capsules
Incorrect Calculation:
1.5g = 150mg (incorrect conversion) → 150mg ÷ 500mg/capsule = 0.3 capsules
Identify and correct the conversion error.

⚠️
LEGAL RESPONSIBILITY
As a medical assistant, you have a legal duty to question any order that appears unsafe. The "Five Rights" of medication administration include the "Right Dose" – if you have doubts about the dose, you must clarify with the prescribing provider before administration.

Quality & Safety: Medication errors are among the most common healthcare errors. Your diligence in dose calculation and verification protects patient safety and meets professional standards.

🩺 Clinical Competency Insights

  • Unit Conversion: Always convert to the same unit before calculating. Memorize: 1g = 1000mg, 1mg = 1000mcg. Double-check conversions – this is where most errors occur.
  • Decimal Placement: Misplaced decimals can cause 10x errors. Use leading zeros (0.5 mg, not .5 mg) and trailing zeros only after decimals (2.0 mg, not 2 mg).
  • Dimensional Analysis: The most reliable method: Desired ÷ Available = Quantity. Cancel units as you go to verify your setup is correct.
  • Reasonableness Check: Ask: "Does this dose make sense?" If calculating 25 tablets for one dose, you've likely made an error. Know typical dose ranges for common medications.
  • Legal/Ethical Duty: You have both a legal and ethical responsibility to question orders that appear incorrect. Use respectful communication: "Doctor, I just want to clarify..."

📋 Documentation & Safety Protocols

  • Verification Process: Always calculate doses independently, then verify with another qualified healthcare professional for high-risk medications (insulin, heparin, chemotherapy).
  • Error Reporting: If an error occurs (or is caught before administration), follow facility protocol for incident reporting. Document facts only, without blame or speculation.
  • Competency Validation: Dosage calculation competency must be validated annually. Maintain records of training and successful completion of calculation tests.
  • Patient Education: When appropriate, explain the medication dose to the patient using clear language. "You'll take half of this white tablet once daily."
