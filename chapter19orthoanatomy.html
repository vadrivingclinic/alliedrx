✓ Fracture Classification Mastered!
Greenstick Fracture: Incomplete break where one side of bone fractures and the other bends; common in children due to more flexible bones.

Comminuted Fracture: Bone breaks into three or more fragments; often caused by high‑impact trauma and usually requires surgical intervention.

Spiral Fracture: Twisting force causes helical break along the bone; common in sports injuries or falls with rotation.

Compound (Open) Fracture: Broken bone penetrates the skin, creating an open wound; high infection risk requiring immediate irrigation, antibiotics, and often surgical debridement.

Clinical Tip: Always assess neurovascular status before and after fracture reduction. Document CMS (circulation, motor, sensation) checks.

✓ Spinal Disorders Matched!
Scoliosis: Lateral (side‑to‑side) curvature of the spine; most common in adolescent females. Screened via forward‑bend test. Treatment ranges from observation to bracing or surgery.

Kyphosis: Excessive outward curvature of the thoracic spine, causing rounded back. Can be postural, Scheuermann's disease, or age‑related (osteoporosis).

Lordosis: Excessive inward curvature of the lumbar spine; associated with obesity, pregnancy, or poor posture. May cause low back pain.

Herniated Disc: Annulus fibrosus tears, allowing nucleus pulposus to extrude and compress spinal nerves. Presents with radicular pain, numbness, or weakness (e.g., sciatica).

Clinical Tip: Always assess for red flags: bowel/bladder dysfunction, progressive weakness, or saddle anesthesia—these require immediate referral.

🦴 Clinical Insights: Orthopedic Assessment

  • Fracture Assessment: Always use the Ottawa rules for imaging decisions. For ankle injuries: image if there is bone tenderness at malleoli or inability to bear weight immediately and in ED.
  • Spinal Screening: School‑based scoliosis screening typically occurs at ages 10–14. A curvature ≥7° on forward‑bend test warrants referral to orthopedics.
  • Neurovascular Checks: Document CMS (circulation, motor, sensation) before and after any manipulation, casting, or surgery. Capillary refill >2 seconds indicates compromised circulation.
  • Patient Education: Teach fracture patients signs of compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, pulselessness (late sign).
  • Documentation: Use precise terminology: "closed" vs. "open" fracture, "displaced" vs. "nondisplaced," and anatomical location (proximal, mid‑shaft, distal).

📋 Orthopedic Documentation & Referral

  • Imaging Orders: X‑ray is first line for acute injury. MRI is reserved for soft‑tissue evaluation (ligaments, tendons, discs). CT better defines complex fractures.
  • Referral Criteria: Refer to orthopedics for: open fractures, neurovascular compromise, joint involvement, significant displacement, or failed conservative treatment.
  • Follow‑up Timing: Non‑displaced fractures: re‑evaluate in 1–2 weeks. Post‑op: first visit at 10–14 days for suture removal and wound check.
  • Prevention Education: Counsel on fall prevention for elderly, proper footwear, weight‑bearing exercise for bone density, and ergonomics for repetitive‑strain injuries.
