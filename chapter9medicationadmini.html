Seven Rights Sequence

Drag the Seven Rights of Medication Administration into the correct sequence. Following these rights in order prevents medication errors and ensures patient safety.

Right Patient - Verify using two identifiers
Right Medication - Check label three times
Right Dose - Calculate and verify appropriate amount
Right Route - Confirm administration method
Right Time - Administer at correct frequency
Right Documentation - Record immediately after administration
Right Reason - Ensure indication matches prescription
⚠️
Medication Error Risk!
Skipping any of the Seven Rights increases error risk by 300%. Always verify patient identity first - wrong patient errors are most dangerous.
✓ Seven Rights Sequence Correct!
Correct Sequence & Rationale:
1. Right Patient - Verify using two identifiers (name, DOB, ID#)
2. Right Medication - Check label 3 times: when removing, preparing, and administering
3. Right Dose - Calculate independently, have second nurse verify high-alert medications
4. Right Route - Confirm method (oral, IV, IM, etc.) - wrong route can be fatal
5. Right Time - Administer within 30 minutes before/after scheduled time
6. Right Documentation - Document immediately after administration, not before
7. Right Reason - Ensure medication matches diagnosis/indication

Additional Rights: Right to Refuse, Right Education, Right Assessment, Right Evaluation

✓ Injection Techniques Mastered!
Injection Guidelines:
• Subcutaneous (SubQ): 45-90° angle, 0.5-1 mL, abdomen (2" from umbilicus), thighs, upper arms
• Intramuscular (IM): 90° angle, up to 3-4 mL, Z-track technique for irritating medications
• Intradermal (ID): 10-15° angle, 0.1 mL, inner forearm, produces wheal

Site Selection Priorities:
1. Ventrogluteal: Safest for adults (away from major nerves/vessels)
2. Vastus Lateralis: Safest for infants/children
3. Deltoid: Small volumes only (max 1 mL)
4. Avoid Dorsogluteal: High risk of sciatic nerve injury

Needle Sizes: SubQ 25-27G ½-⅝", IM 20-23G 1-1½" (based on patient size)

Critical Calculation Rules
• Always double-check calculations with another provider for high-risk medications
• Use mg/kg/day for most pediatric drugs, m² for chemotherapy
• Round appropriately: liquids to nearest 0.1 mL, tablets to nearest half tab when scored
• Verify dose doesn't exceed adult maximum dose
✓ Pediatric Calculations Correct!
Problem Solutions:
1. Amoxicillin: 20 mg/kg/day × 15 kg = 300 mg/day ÷ 3 doses = 100 mg/dose
  100 mg ÷ (125 mg/5 mL) = 100 mg ÷ 25 mg/mL = 4 mL per dose
2. BSA: √[(110 × 20) ÷ 3600] = √[2200 ÷ 3600] = √0.611 = 0.782 m²
  1.5 mg/m² × 0.782 m² = 1.173 mg daily dose
3. Safe Range: 5-7 mg/kg × 25 kg = 125-175 mg/day
  150 mg is within 125-175 mg range → SAFE

Pediatric Dosing Principles:
• Children are NOT small adults - metabolism and distribution differ
• Use weight in kg (never pounds) for calculations
• Consider renal/hepatic function in premature infants
• Always use proper measuring device (oral syringe, not household spoon)

Medication Reconciliation

Identify medication discrepancies, duplicates, and interactions during admission reconciliation. Proper reconciliation prevents 46% of medication errors.

Patient medications on admission. Select all issues:

Lisinopril 10 mg daily for hypertension
Hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily (already in lisinopril/HCTZ combo)
Warfarin 5 mg daily, INR 2.8
Ibuprofen 800 mg TID PRN for arthritis pain
Metformin 1000 mg BID (renal function: CrCl 35 mL/min)
Atorvastatin 40 mg nightly for hyperlipidemia
⚠️
Critical Interaction Alert!
Warfarin + NSAIDs significantly increase bleeding risk. NSAIDs also reduce renal function, affecting metformin clearance.
✓ Reconciliation Issues Identified!
Medication Issues Found:
1. Duplicate Therapy: Hydrochlorothiazide duplicate in lisinopril/HCTZ combo
2. Drug Interaction: Warfarin + ibuprofen = increased bleeding risk
3. Renal Dosing: Metformin contraindicated with CrCl <30 mL/min (patient at 35 - needs monitoring)

Reconciliation Process:
• Best Possible Medication History (BPMH): Verify with patient, family, pharmacy
• Compare: Admission orders vs. home medications
• Reconcile: Resolve discrepancies with provider
• Communicate: Provide updated list at discharge

High-Risk Situations: Transitions of care, multiple providers, high-risk medications (anticoagulants, insulin, opioids), cognitive impairment

Adverse Reaction Response

Sequence emergency response steps for anaphylaxis and understand different types of adverse drug reactions. Proper response timing saves lives.

Stop medication immediately
Assess ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation)
Administer epinephrine IM (anterolateral thigh)
Call rapid response/code team
Position patient supine with legs elevated
Administer oxygen, establish IV access
Document reaction and interventions
Report to FDA MedWatch and pharmacy
Understanding Different Reaction Types
Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are classified into different types based on their mechanism, predictability, and relationship to dose. Understanding these differences helps in prevention, identification, and management.
Allergic Reaction
Examples: Penicillin allergy, NSAID-induced urticaria
• Immune system-mediated response
• Not dose-dependent
• Requires prior exposure (sensitization)
• Can range from mild rash to anaphylaxis
• Treatment: Stop drug, antihistamines, epinephrine for severe
Idiosyncratic Reaction
Examples: Malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome
• Unpredictable, unusual response
• Often genetic predisposition
• Not related to drug's pharmacological action
• Can be severe or life-threatening
• Treatment: Stop drug, supportive care
Side Effect
Examples: Nausea from antibiotics, drowsiness from antihistamines
• Predictable pharmacological effect
• Dose-dependent
• Usually mild to moderate
• Often listed in drug information
• Treatment: Adjust dose or manage symptoms
Toxic Reaction
Examples: Digoxin toxicity, lithium toxicity
• Due to excessive dosing
• Predictable based on drug properties
• Often involves narrow therapeutic index drugs
• Can be life-threatening
• Treatment: Stop drug, specific antidotes if available
✓ Emergency Response Correct!
Anaphylaxis Protocol (FAST):
F - Face (swelling, itching)
A - Airway (tightness, stridor, hoarseness)
S - Stomach (cramping, vomiting)
T - Total body (hives, redness, low BP)

Emergency Response Sequence:
1. STOP medication, call for help
2. EPINEPHRINE 0.3-0.5 mg IM (adult dose)
3. POSITION supine with legs elevated
4. OXYGEN, IV fluids, monitor vitals
5. SECONDARY medications: antihistamines, steroids, bronchodilators
6. DOCUMENT thoroughly: time, symptoms, interventions
7. REPORT to FDA MedWatch (required for severe reactions)

Key Differences in Reaction Types:
• Allergic: Immune-mediated, requires prior exposure
• Idiosyncratic: Unpredictable, often genetic
• Side Effect: Predictable, dose-related
• Toxic: Overdose, narrow therapeutic index

📋 Documentation & Quality Indicators

  • Medication Administration Record (MAR): Document immediately after administration. Include time, dose, route, site (for injections), and your initials. Never document before giving.
  • Error Reporting: Report ALL errors and near-misses. Focus on system improvements, not individual blame. Document objectively: who, what, when, where, how, and contributing factors.
  • High-Alert Medications: Double verification required for insulin, heparin, opioids, chemotherapy. Document both verifiers' names and times.
  • Patient Education Documentation: Document specific teaching provided, materials given, patient questions, and verification of understanding using teach-back method.
