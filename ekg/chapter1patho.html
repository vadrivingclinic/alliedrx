Scenario 1: ST Segment Interpretation

A 58-year-old male presents with 45 minutes of crushing chest pain radiating to his left arm. His ECG shows ST segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF.

The cardiology fellow asks you: "Based on the ECG findings, which coronary artery is most likely occluded, and what area of the heart is experiencing injury?"

Select the correct coronary artery and myocardial region:
Right Coronary Artery (RCA) - Inferior wall
Left Anterior Descending (LAD) - Anterior wall
Circumflex artery - Lateral wall
Left main coronary artery - Extensive anterior/lateral

Scenario 2: Hemodynamic Response

A patient with an acute anterior wall MI develops cardiogenic shock. The intensive care team notes: Blood pressure 85/50, heart rate 120, cool clammy skin, and decreased urine output.

The attending physician asks: "Which hemodynamic parameter is MOST critically affected in cardiogenic shock, leading to this clinical presentation?"

Identify the primary hemodynamic problem:
Severely reduced cardiac contractility
Increased peripheral vascular resistance
Excessive preload from fluid overload
Bradycardia reducing cardiac output

Scenario 3: Reversible vs. Irreversible

During morning report, the charge nurse presents a case: "Patient with 20 minutes of chest pain, ECG shows ST depression in V4-V6. Troponin negative. Given sublingual nitroglycerin with relief of symptoms."

The nurse asks: "Which condition best describes this patient's current state, and is it reversible with appropriate treatment?"

Select the correct diagnosis and reversibility status:
Unstable angina - Reversible ischemia
NSTEMI - Irreversible injury
STEMI - Irreversible infarction
Stable angina - Chronic fixed issue
📚 Key ACS Terminology
UA = Unstable angina (reversible ischemia). NSTEMI = Non-ST elevation MI (injury, troponin+). STEMI = ST elevation MI (infarction, needs emergent reperfusion). All are Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS).

🧠 Key Pathophysiological Insights

  • ACS Progression: Plaque rupture → Thrombus formation → Vasoconstriction → Ischemia (reversible) → Injury (ST changes) → Infarction (necrosis). Time is muscle - reperfusion within 90 minutes maximizes salvage.
  • Ischemia Continuum: Ischemia = O2 demand > supply (reversible). Injury = cellular damage (ST changes, may be reversible). Infarction = cell death (irreversible, Q waves develop).
  • Hemodynamic Determinants: Cardiac Output = Stroke Volume × Heart Rate. SV determined by Preload (filling), Afterload (resistance), Contractility (muscle strength). Understanding these helps manage shock and heart failure.
  • ECG-Anatomy Correlation: Inferior leads (II, III, aVF) = RCA territory. Anterior leads (V1-V4) = LAD territory. Lateral leads (I, aVL, V5-V6) = Circumflex territory. Posterior changes = reciprocal to anterior.
  • Clinical Application: STEMI needs emergent PCI/thrombolytics. NSTEMI needs antiplatelets/anticoagulants. Unstable angina needs medical management. All need risk factor modification.

🎯 Critical Takeaways for EKG Technicians

  • Recognize STEMI: New ST elevation at J point in ≥2 contiguous leads: ≥2mm in men (≥1.5mm in women) in V2-V3, ≥1mm in other leads. This is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY requiring immediate activation of cath lab.
  • Understand Ischemia Patterns: ST depression = subendocardial ischemia. T wave inversion = ischemia. ST elevation = transmural injury. Pathological Q waves = old infarction.
  • Correlate with Symptoms: Always ask about chest pain, radiation, associated symptoms. Document timing and relationship to ECG changes. Silent MIs occur but are less common.
  • Know Reperfusion Timeframes: Door-to-balloon time should be <90 minutes. Every 30-minute delay increases mortality by 7.5%. Your rapid, accurate ECG can save lives.
  • Communicate Effectively: Use clear terminology: "Acute ST elevation in inferior leads consistent with acute inferior wall MI" not just "abnormal ECG." State time of onset if known.
