Core Process

Bulk Repackaging Steps

The systematic process for transferring medications from bulk containers to unit dose packaging:

1
Verify Original Container: Check drug name, strength, lot number, expiration date
2
Prepare Work Area: Clean surface, gather supplies (packaging, labels, counting tray)
3
Calculate BUD: Determine Beyond-Use Date based on USP guidelines and stability
4
Count/Measure: Accurately count tablets or measure liquid using calibrated equipment
5
Package: Place medication in unit dose packaging (blister pack, strip pack, bottle)
6
Label: Apply label with drug name, strength, lot #, exp date, BUD, pharmacy info
7
Document: Complete repackaging logbook with all required information
8
Quality Check: Pharmacist verifies accuracy before medication is dispensed

Quality Control

Beyond-Use Date (BUD) Determination

Calculating Stability and Shelf Life for Repackaged Medications

BUD Definition
Date after which a compounded or repackaged medication should not be used; determined from preparation date
General Rule
BUD cannot exceed manufacturer's original expiration date OR stability-based date (whichever is earlier)
USP Guidelines
Non-sterile compounds: Typically 6 months or less
Repackaged solids: 6 months or 25% of remaining shelf life
Repackaged liquids: Shorter BUDs due to stability concerns
Calculation Example
Original expiration: 12/15/2024
Repackaging date: 06/15/2024
25% of remaining: 3 months (25% of 12 months)
BUD: 09/15/2024 (earlier of 6 months or 25% rule)

Documentation: BUD must be clearly labeled on repackaged medication and recorded in logbook.

Tools & Equipment

Non-Sterile Compounding Equipment

Essential tools for compounding medications (Judy's hands-on training experience):

Mortar & Pestle
For triturating (grinding) tablets into powder; glass (for liquids), porcelain (for powders), wedgewood (for oily substances)
Electronic Balance
Precision scales (mg sensitivity) for weighing APIs; must be calibrated regularly; Class A prescription balance
Graduated Cylinders
For measuring liquids; different sizes for accuracy; read at meniscus level for precision
Capsule Filling Machine
For preparing capsules; punch method for small batches; automated for larger quantities
Ointment Slab/Spatula
Glass or ceramic slab for mixing ointments/creams; flexible spatulas for incorporation
Troche Molds
Calibrated molds for preparing troches (lozenges); require careful calibration for accurate dosing

Scale Types

  • Class A Prescription Balance: Traditional two-pan balance; sensitivity 6 mg
  • Electronic Analytical Balance: Digital readout; sensitivity 0.1 mg or better
  • Top-Loading Balance: Less sensitive but faster; for larger quantities

Compounding Applications

Non-Sterile Dosage Forms

Common dosage forms compounded under USP Chapter 795 (Judy's training experience):

Creams
Water-based (hydrophilic); oil-in-water emulsion; for moisturizing or water-soluble drugs
Ointments
Oil-based (hydrophobic/oleaginous); petroleum jelly bases; for occlusive effects or lipid-soluble drugs
Solutions
Clear liquids with dissolved API; water, alcohol, or oil bases; homogeneous mixture
Suspensions
Undissolved particles in liquid; require "Shake Well" label; may settle over time
Troches
Lozenges that dissolve in mouth; molded dosage form; for local or systemic effect
Capsules
Powder-filled gelatin shells; precise dosing; can combine multiple powders
Elixirs
Sweetened hydro-alcoholic solutions; pleasant tasting; for oral administration
Syrups
Concentrated sugar solutions; viscous; mask bitter drug tastes

Patient Needs: Allergies to commercial products, specific dosage requirements, discontinued medications, pediatric/geriatric dosing.

Specialty Area

Veterinary Compounding

Dosage forms commonly compounded for animal patients:

Flavor-Masked Solutions
Medications compounded with meat, fish, or poultry flavors to improve palatability
Transdermal Gels
Applied to inner ear or skin for systemic absorption in uncooperative animals
Suspensions
Custom concentrations for different sized animals (small dogs to large horses)
Capsules
Precise dosing for specific weights; can be opened and mixed with food
Topicals
Ointments and creams for dermatological conditions; may need bittering agents
Treat Forms
Medications compounded into chewable treats or biscuits for easy administration

Special Considerations: Species-specific metabolism, weight-based dosing, palatability, administration route challenges.

Essential Terminology

API

Process of grinding or crushing powder (tablets) into fine particles using mortar and pestle.
