✓ USP <797> Compliance Analysis
Correct Sequence:
1. Hand hygiene and removal of jewelry/watches
2. Don shoe covers, hair cover, and face mask
3. Perform three-minute surgical scrub of hands and forearms
4. Don sterile gown using aseptic technique
5. Don sterile gloves ensuring no contamination
6. Enter cleanroom through ante-room pressure cascade
7. Disinfect work surface with sterile 70% IPA
8. Perform first air verification and laminar airflow check
9. Begin aseptic compounding following beyond-use dating

Critical Control Points:
• Steps 1, 2, 5 are CRITICAL - failures can directly compromise sterility
• First Air Principle: Nothing should come between HEPA filter and sterile product
• Pressure Cascade: Cleanroom must maintain positive pressure to ante-room

Beyond-Use Dating (BUD):
• Low-risk, room temperature: 48 hours or manufacturer's recommendation
• Medium-risk, refrigerated: 30 days
• High-risk, frozen: 45 days (requires sterility testing)

Risk Management Scenarios

Analyze medication error scenarios and select the appropriate prevention strategies. Develop critical thinking skills for real-world risk management.

⚠️ Safety Committee Preparation
Jonathan is reviewing recent near-miss reports for the pharmacy safety committee. He must analyze each scenario and recommend evidence-based prevention strategies.
Scenario 1: A nurse almost administered Humulin R (regular insulin) instead of Humulin N (NPH insulin) to a diabetic patient. Both vials look identical and were stored next to each other in the medication refrigerator.
A) Move the insulin vials to separate shelves in the same refrigerator
B) Add colored stickers to differentiate the vials
C) Implement barcode scanning and store in separate, labeled bins
D) Require double-check by another nurse for all insulin administrations
Best Practice: Option C addresses the root cause through multiple layers:
1. Barcode scanning provides point-of-care verification
2. Separate storage prevents mix-ups during retrieval
3. Labeled bins create visual differentiation

LASA (Look-Alike/Sound-Alike) Protocol: Institute "Tall Man" lettering (humuLIN R, humuLIN N) and separate storage as required by ISMP.
Scenario 2: A pharmacy technician prepared a heparin infusion with 25,000 units/mL instead of 1,000 units/mL. The concentration wasn't verified against the order, and the infusion pump wasn't programmed with dose limits.
A) Implement smart pump technology with hard dosing limits
B) Require pharmacist verification of all high-alert medications
C) Standardize to one heparin concentration throughout the hospital
D) Use barcode verification during both preparation and administration
Best Practices: Options A & D provide multi-layered protection:
1. Smart pumps with hard limits prevent dangerous infusion rates
2. Barcode verification catches concentration errors at multiple points
3. High-alert protocol requires independent double-check (also important)

ISMP High-Alert Recommendations: Heparin requires independent double-check, barcode verification, and smart pump limits as part of a comprehensive safety strategy.
Scenario 3: A patient received a medication they were allergic to because the allergy wasn't documented in the EHR. The patient had told the nurse during admission, but it wasn't entered into the system.
A) Require pharmacists to verbally confirm allergies with all patients
B) Implement EHR alerts and standardized allergy documentation protocol
C) Use clinical decision support with cross-reaction checking
D) Add allergy wristbands for all patients with documented allergies
Best Practices: Options B & C address system-level solutions:
1. Standardized documentation ensures consistency across departments
2. EHR alerts provide real-time warnings at prescribing and dispensing
3. Clinical decision support checks for cross-reactions and contraindications

NCC MERP Recommendations: Technology should support, not replace, the medication reconciliation process at all care transitions.
Scenario 4: A chemotherapy dose was calculated incorrectly based on body surface area. The error wasn't caught because there was no independent verification process for chemotherapy orders.
A) Require dual verification by pharmacist and second pharmacist
B) Implement calculator software for all dose calculations
C) Use computerized order entry with dose-range checking
D) Standardize all chemotherapy protocols to fixed doses
Best Practices: Options A & C create multiple safety layers:
1. Dual verification is required for all chemotherapy by ASHP standards
2. CPOE with dose checking prevents calculation and transcription errors
3. Standardized protocols help but cannot replace verification for individualized dosing

Oncology Safety Standards: Chemotherapy requires specialized training, double-checking, and separate preparation areas to prevent life-threatening errors.
✓ Risk Management Analysis
Key Risk Management Principles:
• Swiss Cheese Model: Multiple layers of defense prevent errors from reaching patients
• Root Cause Analysis (RCA): Identify system issues, not individual blame
• Failure Mode & Effects Analysis (FMEA): Proactively identify potential failures

Technician's Role in Risk Management:
1. Documentation: Accurate recording of near-misses and errors
2. Monitoring: Regular checks of high-alert medication storage and labeling
3. Reporting: Non-punitive reporting of safety concerns
4. Verification: Double-checking critical calculations and preparations

Quality Improvement Cycle:
Plan → Do → Study → Act (PDSA) for continuous safety improvement

🏥 USP <797> Clinical Applications

  • Critical Compounding Areas: Beyond-use dating varies by risk level: Low-risk compounds (48 hours), Medium-risk (30 days refrigerated), High-risk (requires sterility testing). Technicians must verify risk level before assigning BUD.
  • Environmental Monitoring: Daily pressure differential checks, monthly particle counts, and annual HEPA filter certification are required. Technicians often perform initial monitoring and must document excursions immediately.
  • Garbing Competency: Annual media-fill testing validates aseptic technique. Technicians must pass with zero growth to maintain compounding privileges. Documentation of competency is audited during Joint Commission surveys.

🛡️ Risk Management Protocols

  • High-Alert Medications: ISMP identifies 19 classes requiring special safeguards. Technicians implement "Tall Man" lettering, separate storage, and auxiliary labeling. Independent double-checks are required before dispensing.
  • LASA Management: Look-alike/sound-alike drugs require separation by at least 3 feet or physical barriers. Computer systems should include "Do Not Confuse" alerts. Technicians verify correct drug selection during order fulfillment.
  • REMS Programs: Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies for drugs like isotretinoin, clozapine, and opioids. Technicians verify enrollment, document counseling, and monitor dispensing limits as part of the safety protocol.

👨‍⚕️ Pharmacy Technician Safety Role

  • Sterility Assurance: Technicians perform 70% of sterile compounding in hospitals. Their attention to detail in garbing, surface disinfection, and first air maintenance directly impacts patient infection rates.
  • Error Reporting: Technicians report 80% of dispensing near-misses. Their documentation provides data for root cause analysis and system improvements. Non-punitive reporting culture is essential for accurate data.
  • Quality Control: Daily balance calibration, refrigerator temperature logs, and equipment maintenance are technician responsibilities. These quality checks prevent errors before they reach patients.
