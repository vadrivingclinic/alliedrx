Pharmacokinetics (ADME Process)
Absorption
Drug enters bloodstream from administration site
Distribution
Drug circulates to tissues and target sites
Metabolism
Liver breaks down drug (first-pass effect)
Elimination
Kidneys remove drug from body (urine)
Key Pharmacological Terms
Pharmacokinetics
What the body does to the drug (ADME)
Pharmacodynamics
What the drug does to the body (effects)
Half-life (t½)
Time for drug concentration to decrease by 50%
Bioavailability
% of administered dose reaching systemic circulation
Bioequivalence
Similar bioavailability between generic and brand
First-pass Effect
Liver metabolism before systemic distribution
Excipients
Inactive ingredients in medications
Legend Drugs
Require prescription ("Rx only")
OTC Drugs
Over-the-counter, no prescription needed
BTC Drugs
Behind-the-counter, limited pharmacy sales

Clinical Foundations

Why Dosage Forms & Pharmacokinetics Matter

Chapter 5 provides the scientific foundation for understanding how medications work in the body. Knowing dosage forms, routes, and pharmacokinetics isn't just academic— it's essential for safe medication preparation, patient counseling, and error prevention.

Clinical Applications:

  • Error Prevention: Knowing dangerous abbreviations prevents medication errors.
  • Patient Education: Understanding routes helps explain proper administration.
  • Drug Storage: Proper handling maintains medication efficacy and safety.
  • Generic Substitution: Understanding bioequivalence ensures therapeutic equivalence.
Learning Objectives Integration:
  • Objective 1: Dangerous abbreviations & TJC "Do Not Use" list
  • Objective 2-4: Drug classifications, dosage forms, administration routes
  • Objective 5-7: Pharmacokinetics vs. pharmacodynamics, ADME, half-life
  • Objective 8-9: Excipients, drug storage requirements
  • Objective 10: Medical terminology and abbreviations
First-Pass Effect Importance:
  • Oral drugs pass through liver before systemic circulation
  • Can reduce bioavailability significantly (e.g., nitroglycerin)
  • Explains why some drugs require non-oral routes
  • Affects dosing calculations and administration choices
  • Key consideration in drug formulation development

Drug Classifications by Body System

  • Cardiovascular: Antihypertensives, antiarrhythmics, anticoagulants
  • Central Nervous System: Analgesics, antidepressants, anticonvulsants
  • Gastrointestinal: Antacids, antiemetics, laxatives
  • Endocrine: Insulin, thyroid hormones, corticosteroids
  • Respiratory: Bronchodilators, antihistamines, corticosteroids
Storage Requirements Examples:
  • Refrigeration (2-8°C): Insulin, some antibiotics, biologicals
  • Room Temperature (15-30°C): Most tablets and capsules
  • Light Protection: Nitroglycerin, some vitamins, light-sensitive drugs
  • Moisture Protection: Effervescent tablets, certain capsules
  • Controlled Substances: Locked storage, specific documentation

Excipients & Drug Formulation

  • Binders: Hold tablets together (cellulose, starches)
  • Fillers/Diluents: Add bulk to formulations (lactose, calcium phosphate)
  • Disintegrants: Help tablets break down in GI tract (croscarmellose)
  • Lubricants: Prevent sticking during manufacturing (magnesium stearate)
  • Preservatives: Prevent microbial growth (benzyl alcohol, parabens)
  • Flavorings/Colorings: Improve patient acceptance
Orange Book & Bioequivalence:
  • FDA's Approved Drug Products with Therapeutic Equivalence Evaluations
  • Lists generic drugs bioequivalent to brand-name counterparts
  • Codes indicate therapeutic equivalence ratings (AB rated = bioequivalent)
  • Essential reference for generic substitution decisions
  • Ensures patient receives therapeutically equivalent medication
