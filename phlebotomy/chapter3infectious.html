🦠 Bloodborne Pathogens Identification

Identify and understand the three primary bloodborne pathogens that pose significant risks to phlebotomists. Learn their characteristics, transmission methods, and clinical significance.

Hepatitis B (HBV)
Virus Type: DNA virus
Incubation: 60-150 days
Survival: 7 days on surfaces
Vaccine: Available & recommended
Risk: Needlestick transmission risk: 6-30%
Hepatitis C (HCV)
Virus Type: RNA virus
Incubation: 14-180 days
Survival: Less stable than HBV
Vaccine: No vaccine available
Risk: Needlestick transmission risk: 1.8%
HIV
Virus Type: Retrovirus
Incubation: 2-4 weeks (acute)
Survival: Hours in dried blood
Vaccine: No vaccine available
Risk: Needlestick transmission risk: 0.3%
💡 Clinical Insight
HBV is the most stable and infectious bloodborne pathogen. It can survive on environmental surfaces for up to 7 days and is transmitted 50-100 times more easily than HIV. This is why vaccination is CRITICAL for all healthcare workers.

⚠️
OSHA BLOODBORNE PATHOGENS STANDARD
Employers MUST provide: (1) HBV vaccination series, (2) Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), (3) Training on bloodborne pathogens, (4) Post-exposure evaluation and follow-up. Non-compliance can result in fines up to $70,000 per violation.
✓ Bloodborne Pathogens Mastered!
Key Takeaways:
1. HBV is the most infectious - survives 7 days, transmission risk 6-30%
2. HCV has no vaccine - emphasizes need for strict precautions
3. HIV risk is lower but consequences are severe
4. Vaccination + Standard Precautions = Primary prevention strategy

Clinical Application: Always treat ALL blood and body fluids as potentially infectious. This universal approach protects you from known AND unknown pathogens.

🔄 Transmission Routes & Prevention Matching

Match each transmission route with its correct prevention strategy. Drag and drop the prevention methods into the correct drop zones next to each transmission route.

🩸 Bloodborne Transmission
Pathogens transmitted through contact with blood or body fluids containing visible blood.
Examples: Needlesticks, blood splashes to mucous membranes, contaminated sharps
🤲 Direct Contact
Transmission through physical contact between infected person and susceptible host.
Examples: MRSA skin-to-skin contact, herpes simplex, scabies
💦 Droplet Transmission
Large respiratory droplets (>5μm) traveling short distances (≤3 feet/1 meter).
Examples: Influenza, pertussis, meningococcal disease, COVID-19
🌬️ Airborne Transmission
Small droplet nuclei (<5μm) remaining suspended in air, traveling long distances.
Examples: Tuberculosis, measles, chickenpox, possibly COVID-19 in certain procedures
🩺 Phlebotomy-Specific Risks
Highest risk during: (1) Needle recapping, (2) Transferring blood to tubes, (3) Handling used needles/sharps, (4) Cleaning up blood spills.

Prevention: Never recap needles, use safety-engineered devices, immediate disposal in sharps containers, proper spill cleanup with EPA-registered disinfectants.

Critical Distinctions:
1. Bloodborne: Specific to blood/body fluids → Engineering controls
2. Contact: Direct/indirect contact → Hand hygiene, gloves
3. Droplet: ≤3 feet, large droplets → Surgical mask, distance
4. Airborne: Long distances, small particles → N95, negative pressure

Phlebotomy Application: Always check precaution signs on patient doors. If uncertain, ask nursing staff before entering. Your safety comes first!

🚨
CHAIN BREAK = INFECTION PREVENTION
Breaking ANY link in the chain stops infection transmission. Phlebotomists primarily break the chain at: (1) Mode of Transmission (PPE, safe devices), (2) Portal of Entry (no needle recapping), and (3) Susceptible Host (vaccinations).
💉 Real-World Application: Needlestick Scenario
Scenario: During a routine draw, you get a needlestick from a patient with unknown HIV status.

Chain Analysis:
1. Infectious Agent: Possible HIV
2. Reservoir: Patient's bloodstream
3. Portal of Exit: Blood on contaminated needle
4. Mode of Transmission: Percutaneous injury
5. Portal of Entry: Your bloodstream via needlestick
6. Susceptible Host: You (unless on PEP prophylaxis)

Where could chain have been broken? Safety device, proper disposal, no recapping.
✓ Chain of Infection Mastered!
Correct Chain Sequence:
1. Infectious Agent → 2. Reservoir → 3. Portal of Exit → 4. Mode of Transmission → 5. Portal of Entry → 6. Susceptible Host

Phlebotomy-Specific Interventions:
• Transmission: Safety devices, no needle recapping, proper disposal
• Portal of Entry: Intact skin (no cuts), PPE (gloves, face protection)
• Susceptible Host: Vaccinations (HBV), good nutrition, adequate rest

Remember: You control multiple break points every time you perform phlebotomy. Each safe practice is a deliberate break in the chain.

🩺 Phlebotomy Safety Insights

  • HBV is the Greatest Threat: Most stable, most infectious bloodborne pathogen. Vaccination is non-negotiable for phlebotomists. Document your vaccination records.
  • Transmission Route Determines PPE: Bloodborne = gloves, face protection. Droplet = surgical mask + distance. Airborne = N95 + negative pressure room. Contact = gloves + gown.
  • Chain Breaks Save Lives: Every safe practice breaks the infection chain. Most effective breaks: Engineering controls (safety devices) > Administrative controls (policies) > PPE.
  • Standard Precautions are UNIVERSAL: Apply to ALL patients, ALL the time. Assume ALL blood and body fluids are infectious. This mindset prevents complacency.
  • Post-Exposure Protocol: Know it BEFORE you need it. Immediate washing, reporting, baseline testing, and follow-up are time-sensitive. Seconds matter for some exposures.

📋 Professional & Legal Responsibilities

  • OSHA Compliance: You have the right to a safe workplace. Employers must provide training, PPE, vaccinations, and exposure follow-up. Report deficiencies through proper channels.
  • Documentation: Accurate records protect you legally. Document training completion, vaccination dates, exposure incidents, and post-exposure follow-up.
  • Quality Indicators: Low needlestick rates = good technique + safety culture. High rates indicate systemic problems needing intervention.
  • Patient Education: Explain safety measures to patients. "I'm wearing gloves to protect both of us." This builds trust and models infection control.
