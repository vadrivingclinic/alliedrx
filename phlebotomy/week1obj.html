Foundations of Phlebotomy Practice

Professional Standards • Human Anatomy • Blood Collection Basics

Week 1 establishes the essential knowledge base for your phlebotomy career. You'll begin with professional standards and ethics, then dive deep into human anatomy and physiology with special focus on the circulatory system.

These two chapters are interconnected because anatomy knowledge directly informs safe phlebotomy practice. Understanding vein structure, blood composition, and physiological principles enables you to collect specimens effectively while minimizing patient discomfort and risk.

👨‍⚕️ Chapter 1 Scenario: The Multidisciplinary Team
Setting: Large metropolitan hospital with 500+ beds
Team Members: Nurses, Medical Assistants, Lab Technicians, Respiratory Therapists
Challenge: Understanding who can perform phlebotomy in different states and settings, and how certification vs. licensure affects scope of practice.
🏥 Chapter 2 Scenario: Anatomy in Action
Patient: 68-year-old male with difficult venous access
Challenge: Identifying alternative venipuncture sites beyond the median cubital vein
Solution: Applying knowledge of venous anatomy, vessel depth, and adjacent structures to safely access the basilic or cephalic veins.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • Identify which healthcare team members can perform phlebotomy in various clinical settings.
  • Explain the ethical and legal standards governing phlebotomy practice.
  • Name and locate the three primary veins used for venipuncture.
  • Describe blood circulation pathways and vessel structures.
  • Differentiate blood components and explain their clinical significance.

Phlebotomy Insight: The median cubital vein is the preferred venipuncture site because it's typically large, well-anchored, and has fewer nerves nearby, reducing patient discomfort during blood draws.
Anatomy Tip: When locating veins, remember: "cephalic" = thumb side (lateral), "basilic" = pinky side (medial), and "median cubital" connects them in the antecubital fossa.
Professional Development: Most states don't require phlebotomist licensure, but certification significantly increases employment opportunities and average salary. Major certifying organizations include ASCP, NHA, and AMT.
