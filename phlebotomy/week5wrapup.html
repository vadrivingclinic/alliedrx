Step 2

Newborn Screening Tests

Common tests performed on newborn infants. Understanding these helps minimize blood draws and prevent iatrogenic anemia.

Phenylketonuria (PKU)
Tests for phenylalanine levels to detect metabolic disorder
Galactosemia
Tests for galactose metabolism disorder
Thyroid Function (TSH)
Screens for congenital hypothyroidism
Hemoglobinopathies
Screens for sickle cell disease and other hemoglobin disorders
Bilirubin
Monitors for jaundice and liver function
Blood Cultures
If infection (sepsis) is suspected
Critical Consideration: Newborns have small blood volumes (80-100 mL/kg). Excessive draws can cause iatrogenic anemia. Always use microcollection techniques and coordinate draws between departments.

Step 3

Key Vocabulary Flashcards

Select each card to reveal the definition. These terms are essential for understanding special populations and complications.

Iatrogenic Anemia
Anemia caused by medical treatment, particularly excessive blood draws in newborns or critically ill patients.
Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn
Condition where maternal antibodies attack fetal red blood cells, often requiring monitoring through blood tests.
Dementia
Progressive cognitive decline affecting memory, thinking, and behavior; common in geriatric patients requiring special communication approaches.
Hematoma
A collection of blood outside blood vessels, often caused by needle penetration through vein walls during venipuncture.
Hemolysis
Rupture of red blood cells, which can occur from improper technique (vigorous shaking, small needle, drawing from hematoma).
Syncope
Fainting or loss of consciousness, a common complication during venipuncture often related to anxiety or vasovagal response.

Age-Specific Considerations

Neonatal vs. Geriatric Phlebotomy

Consideration Neonatal (Newborns) Geriatric (Older Adults)
Primary Sites Heel stick (plantar surfaces), occasionally venous if large volume needed Antecubital veins (may be fragile), hand veins, forearm veins
Skin & Vein Characteristics Thin skin, minimal subcutaneous fat, delicate veins Thin, fragile skin, loss of elasticity, veins may roll or collapse
Blood Volume Concerns Very limited total volume (iatrogenic anemia risk) May have reduced volume, dehydration common
Communication Approach Parent/guardian communication, gentle handling Clear, simple instructions, may need repetition, address hearing/vision impairments
Common Challenges Minimizing pain, preventing nerve/bone damage, coordinating multiple tests Arthritis limitations, dementia, multiple medications, chronic conditions

Scenario Reflection

You need to draw blood from an 85-year-old patient with arthritis in both hands, mild dementia, and very fragile veins.

Step 4

Common Complications & Solutions

Prevention and Management

Hematoma (Bruising)
Cause: Needle through vein wall, insufficient pressure
Prevention: Proper needle angle, adequate pressure (3-5 minutes), avoid probing
Management: Apply pressure, ice pack, elevate limb
Syncope (Fainting)
Cause: Anxiety, vasovagal response, fear of needles
Prevention: Have patient seated/laying, conversation distraction, monitor for pallor/sweating
Management: Remove needle immediately, lower head, call for help if unresponsive
Hemolysis
Cause: Vigorous mixing, small needle, drawing from hematoma, pulling syringe plunger too fast
Prevention: Gentle tube mixing, proper needle size, avoid difficult draws
Management: Redraw specimen if possible, document occurrence
Failed Draw (No Blood Return)
Cause: Collapsed vein, bevel against wall, needle dislodged
Prevention: Proper tourniquet, good anchoring, correct angle
Management: Release tourniquet, withdraw slightly, adjust angle, try different site if needed
Pro Tips: Always have emergency equipment accessible. Document all complications accurately. Never attempt more than 2 venipunctures on one patient—seek assistance or alternative collection methods.
