🧪 POCT vs Central Lab Testing

Categorize each test based on where it's typically performed. This helps understand which tests phlebotomists might perform at the point-of-care.

📋 Clinical Setting: Emergency Department
You're working as a multiskilled phlebotomist in the ED. The nurse needs rapid results for several patients. Which tests can you perform at bedside (POCT) vs which must go to the central lab?
✓ Testing Location Analysis
Key Distinctions:
• POCT Tests: Blood glucose, Rapid Strep A, Urinalysis dipstick, Hemoglobin by cuvette, Pregnancy test, INR on coagulation monitor
• Central Lab Tests: Complete Blood Count (CBC), Blood culture, Hemoglobin A1c, Comprehensive Metabolic Panel

Why This Matters: POCT provides immediate results for time-sensitive decisions (hypoglycemia, UTI, pregnancy). Central lab tests require specialized equipment, processing, or incubation time.

Clinical Insight: As a multiskilled phlebotomist, you'll perform waived POCT tests. Knowing which tests are POCT vs central lab helps you manage workflow and set appropriate patient expectations.

Pro Tip: Always check your facility's CLIA waiver list to know exactly which POCT tests you're authorized to perform.

⚠️ Quality Control Priority Scenario

Sequence the correct order for performing a glucose test. This demonstrates why quality control comes BEFORE patient testing.

🏥 Scenario: Morning Glucometer Testing
It's 7:00 AM. You need to perform glucose tests on three patients before breakfast. The glucometer was used yesterday and seemed fine. What's the correct sequence of steps to ensure accurate results?
✓ Quality Control Priority
Correct Order:
1. Perform quality control on the glucometer
2. Verify patient identity
3. Select the correct test strip and ensure glucometer is coded
4. Perform the fingerstick and apply blood to test strip
5. Report the result to the nurse

Critical Point: Quality control MUST come first, even if the device "seemed fine yesterday." QC ensures the instrument is working correctly before any patient testing.

Patient Safety Rationale: Skipping QC could mean:
• Inaccurate high/low readings
• Wrong insulin dosing decisions
• Missed hypoglycemia detection
• Unnecessary treatment

Documentation Note: Always document QC results with date, time, lot numbers, and your initials. This creates an audit trail for patient safety.

🔬 Point-of-Care Testing Insights

  • Waived Testing Definition: POCT tests are often "waived" under CLIA, meaning they have simple procedures with low risk of error. Examples: glucose, hemoglobin, urine dipsticks, rapid strep.
  • Turnaround Time Advantage: POCT provides results in 2-15 minutes vs hours for central lab. This is critical for STAT situations like hypoglycemia, coagulation monitoring, or pregnancy determination in trauma.
  • Common POCT Errors: Most errors come from improper technique: insufficient blood sample, incorrect timing, expired strips, or failure to perform QC. Proper training reduces error rates by 80%.

🛡️ Quality Control Best Practices

  • Daily QC is Non-Negotiable: Every POCT device requires daily QC before patient use. Document every QC result, including date, time, lot numbers, and operator initials.
  • QC Failure Protocol: If QC fails: 1) Don't use the device for patients, 2) Perform troubleshooting, 3) Repeat QC with new controls, 4) If still failing, remove device from service and notify supervisor.
  • Legal Protection: Proper QC documentation protects you legally. If a patient outcome is questioned, your QC records prove you verified device accuracy before testing.

⚠️ Critical Safety Alert

  • Never Skip QC: Even if you're busy, even if the device worked yesterday, even if you "just did it" - QC comes FIRST. A 5-minute QC check prevents potentially fatal errors.
  • Patient Harm Examples: Undetected hypoglycemia → seizure; inaccurate INR → bleeding risk; false pregnancy test → inappropriate medication or imaging. QC prevents these outcomes.
  • Your Responsibility: As the testing operator, YOU are responsible for verifying device accuracy through QC. This responsibility cannot be delegated or assumed.
